# Cochrane's Writing Lessons — Operational

Source: John H. Cochrane, "Writing Tips for Ph. D. Students" (June 2005).

## Words

- **Simple short words, not big fancy ones.** "Use" not "utilize". "Several" not "diverse". "People" not "agents". "Variety" not "diverse".
- **No adjectives for your own work.** "Striking results", "very significant", "very novel" — the world will give you adjectives if they're earned. And no double adjectives.
- **No abbreviated author names.** "Fama and French", not "FF".
- **"Where" = a place; "in which" = a model.** "Models in which consumers have uninsured shocks", not "models where…".
- **Hyphens (JFE rule):** use for true compound modifiers before a noun (after-tax income, risk-free rate, two-day return, value-weighted index). Drop when modifier ends in -ly (publicly traded stock, equally weighted index). Optional when no risk of misreading — but be consistent. No "small-stocks".
- **Italics:** don't overuse. If you need italics for emphasis, often the sentence should be rewritten so the emphasis is structural.
- **Greek letters:** define clearly in an easy-to-find place; give them a name (e.g. "the elasticity of substitution θ"); periodically remind reader of the name. The one place a little repetition is fine.

## Sentences

- **Subject–verb–object.** "The insurance mechanisms that agents utilize to smooth consumption…are diverse" → "People use a variety of insurance mechanisms to smooth consumption."
- **Every sentence needs subject, verb, object.** "No sentences like this." ← the kind of thing not to write.
- **Active voice.** Search the document for "is" and "are" to root out passives. Not: "it is assumed that τ = 3", "data were constructed as follows".
- **Present tense, consistent.** "Fama and French 1993 find that…". Describe what you find in Table 5, not what you will find. Don't switch tense within a paragraph.
- **"I" is fine.** No royal "we" on a sole-authored paper. Use "we" only to mean "you and I"; "you" for the reader. "Table 5 presents estimates" is acceptable.
- **Each sentence must say something and mean what it says.** Strive for precision.
- **Everything before "that" is usually deletable.**
  - "It should be noted that…" → just say it.
  - "It is easy to show that…" usually means it isn't.
  - "A comment is in order at this point." → just make the comment.
  - Search for "that" to clean these up.
- **Clothe the naked "this".** "This shows…" → "This regression shows…". Always put a noun after "this".
- **Keep the number of clauses down.** Don't leave many things hanging.
- **Sign descriptions:** one direction is enough. "When Jane goes up (down) on the teeter-totter, Billy goes down (up)" → drop the parentheticals; add "and vice versa" if needed.
- **Strike "I leave x for future research."**
- **Never write "illustrative test" / "illustrative empirical work."**
- **Don't "assume" model features.** "Consumers have power utility" — not "I assume…". Save "assume" for things that modify reality (e.g. "I assume there are no shifts in the demand curve so the regression of price on quantity identifies supply").

## Paragraphs / micro-organisation

- **Order within a paragraph:** first describe what you do, then explain it, then compare it to alternatives. At the micro level too.
  - Example: "I adjust income by the square root of household size." THEN explain why adjusting matters. THEN compare adjustment functions. Most writers do this in reverse.
- **Don't repeat.** "In other words" is a sign of trouble — go back and say it once, right.
- **No throat-clearing first sentences.** "Financial economists have long wondered…" / "The literature has long been interested in…" / long policy motivation — all wasted space.
- **Previews and recalls signal poor organisation.** "As we will see in Table 6", "Recall from section 2", "this previews section 4" — usually mean things are in the wrong order.

## Footnotes

- **Not for parenthetical comments.** Important → in text. Not important → delete. Parenthetical footnotes mean ideas aren't organised.
- **Use only for what the typical reader can skip but a few might want:** long reference lists, simple algebra, documentation.
- Lots of parentheses are just as bad as lots of footnotes.

## Tables

- **Self-contained captions** — but don't go nuts (some captions are longer than the paper).
- **Regression-table captions** should give the regression equation and variable names (especially LHS).
- **No number in a table that isn't discussed in the text.** "Row 1 of Table 3 shows a u-shaped pattern" is fine. "Table 5 shows summary statistics." (period) is not.
- **Correct number of significant digits**, not whatever the program prints. 4.56783 (s.e. 0.6789) → 4.6 (s.e. 0.7). Two to three sig figs is plenty. Every important number with a standard error.
- **Sensible units.** Percentages good. 2.3 better than 0.0000023.

## Sections (the macro skeleton)

- **Abstract:** 100–150 words. Communicate the central novel contribution. Say what you find, not what you look for. No literature.
- **Introduction:** ≤ 3 pages. First sentence states what *you do*, with a fact ("In a regression of x on y, controlling for z, the coefficient is q"), not philosophy. Roadmap paragraph optional — Cochrane skips it.
- **Literature review:** *after* the contribution. Separate, skippable section. Set off against 2–3 closest papers — not a JEL-style review. Be generous in citations; you don't need to claim others got it wrong.
- **Body:** "There should be nothing before the main result that a reader does not need to know in order to understand the main result."
- **Theory:** the minimum needed to follow the empirics. Don't write a general model and then specialise — write only the specialised model you take to data.
- **Empirical work:** start with the main result. No warm-ups, no extensive description of well-known datasets, no preliminary estimates, no replications, no motivation built from your failures. Then graphs/tables that build intuition. Robustness limited; most belongs in a web appendix.
- **Conclusions:** short. Don't restate findings (already in abstract, intro, body — three times is plenty). Brief acknowledgement of limitations is fine. No grant-application future-research lists. No speculation.
- **Appendices:** the dumping ground for general models, lit comments, all 57 robustness exercises. "Eventually you'll dump them out of the appendix too."
