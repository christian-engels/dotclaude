# Jacobsen (2015) — Operational Writing Rules

Mechanical writing advice from Ben Jacobsen, *Some Research and Writing Tips, Part 2: Writing* (SSRN 2552669, 2015), organised by scale of text.

## Word level

- **No acronyms.** Use search-and-replace; force yourself to write the full term — it makes you find shorter natural variants ("Return Dispersion" instead of CSRD).
- **Plain English over jargon.** "Oil price changes predict stock market returns", not "lagged effect between stock market returns and price changes of oil".
- **Don't name techniques in prose** unless the paper is about techniques (no "OLS", no "GARCH" in your story sentences).
- **Be precise with modifiers**: "oil price *changes*" not "oil prices".
- **Use "I" or "we"** — there is nothing wrong with either. Don't go passive to avoid them.

## Sentence level

- **Active voice, almost always.** Passive is harder to read and imprecise. ("It is generally thought that…" hides who is doing the thinking.)
- **Anything before "that" can usually go.** Search every "that"; delete the prefix and re-read. "We find that oil price changes predict stock returns" → "Oil price changes predict stock returns."
- **Cut "In this paper…"** — yes, where else?
- **Don't state what's obvious from context** ("statistically significant" — obviously, or you wouldn't report it).
- **Halve the length.** Most authors can cut their text by ~50% and say the same thing.
- **Vary sentence structure** — don't reuse the same shape for every result.
- **Examples first, then generalise** — concepts land better through an example.

## Paragraph level

- **First sentence of the abstract = the punch line** (the main finding). Most important sentence in the paper.
- **No throat-clearing opening paragraph.** If you do open with one, every claim must be defensible with a source. Otherwise cut it.
- **Show, don't tell.** Open in the middle of the action — "Laws prohibiting insider trading came late to Germany" — not with context-setting.
- **Research question + main result by paragraph 2 at the latest.** No exceptions.
- **Give magnitudes, not just significance.** "An oil price shock of one s.d. (~10%) predictably lowers world market returns by 1%" — not "we find a significant effect".
- **Avoid repetition; if you must repeat, signal you know you are.**
- **Justify every choice that could look arbitrary** ("data starts in 1715 because that is when the series begin"). If genuinely arbitrary, say so and announce the robustness check.
