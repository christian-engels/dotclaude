## Status and Use

Use this framework as an expert-derived diagnostic. It is not a validated
predictor of publication, citations, awards, policy influence, or social impact.
Its concepts are distilled from the fixed corpus documented in
`corpus-provenance.md`.

Keep that diagnostic corpus separate from candidate-specific comparator
evidence. For a particular assessment, closest-work evidence may be supplied by
the user or discovered and verified through `search-literature`; neither route
updates the framework.

Judge two questions separately:

1. Does the project address a question of sufficiently broad interest?
2. Does it make a sufficient leap over the closest literature?

Do not average the answers. A fashionable, consequential topic can still produce
an incremental paper; a highly original move can still concern something too
minor to warrant broad attention. Importance, interestingness, and validity are
distinct, and interestingness amplifies rather than replaces an important,
credible question (Bartunek, Rynes, and Ireland, 2006, PDF pp. 2–6). This skill
assumes validity and does not evaluate it.

## Criterion One: Sufficiently Broad Interest

Broad interest is not the size or fashionability of the topic. It is the breadth
and consequence of the specific uncertainty the project resolves.

### Identify the affected audiences

Name the primary scholarly audience and any secondary users: researchers,
households, firms, intermediaries, regulators, policymakers, or other
institutions. Interestingness depends on what a particular audience already
believes, while importance depends on what changes for that audience if the
question is answered (Davis, 1971, PDF pp. 1, 3–5, 19–26; Bartunek, Rynes, and
Ireland, 2006, PDF pp. 2–6).

Ask:

- Who should care about the answer?
- What belief, decision, allocation, or research practice belongs to that
  audience?
- Is the proposed audience broader than the authors and the smallest specialist
  niche?

Do not require universal appeal. A question can be broad within an important
literature if it changes a consequential part of that conversation (Colquitt and
George, 2011, PDF p. 1).

### State the informed prior and the consequence of changing it

Require a concrete prior rather than a vague claim that little is known. A result
has broader interest when it materially updates a major theory, a real economic
outcome, a consequential managerial or policy choice, or a future synthesis of
the field (Edmans, 2025, PDF pp. 3–5).

Ask:

- What would an informed reader believe before this project?
- What would the reader understand, predict, or do differently afterward?
- Would the result belong in a future survey or alter a first-order debate?

Audience surprise can help locate a live question, but it is an attention
diagnostic rather than proof of truth, importance, or usefulness (Davis, 1971,
PDF pp. 1–5; Tihanyi, 2020, PDF pp. 1–3).

### Establish scientific or economic stakes

Require a named stake in the specific question: a material outcome, affected
population, contested mechanism, institutional choice, or scientific bottleneck.
Important research connects theory to consequential problems and to plausible
users or beneficiaries (Tihanyi, 2020, PDF pp. 1–3; Howard-Grenville, Vasudeva,
and Yiu, 2022, PDF pp. 1–6).

Ask:

- What is materially at stake if the current understanding is wrong?
- Is the outcome economically or scientifically first-order?
- Does the question concern benefits and costs when the claim is about net value?

A salient context does not automatically make the focal relationship important
(Edmans, 2025, PDF p. 15).

### Test scope beyond the immediate instance

Broad interest usually requires a reason the insight travels beyond one sample,
country, event, platform, or institutional detail. The route can be a general
mechanism, boundary condition, reusable measure, class of problems, or
cross-setting implication. Elevating a one-off task into a class, method, or
capability makes it more cumulative (Kaiser/Hamming, 2010, PDF pp. 8–9).

Ask:

- What class of settings or decisions does the project illuminate?
- Which parts of the claim are setting-specific, and which travel?
- If the case is intrinsically important, why is the event-specific answer itself
  worth knowing?

An unusual topic is more promising when it can change understanding beyond its
immediate setting and grow into a question-led agenda (Niederle, 2011, PDF
pp. 1–3).

### Demand utility, not only attention

Scientific utility includes clearer constructs, mechanisms, predictions,
boundaries, researchable questions, or reusable capabilities. Practical utility
includes better understanding or decisions for actors confronting a consequential
problem. Originality and utility are distinct; revelatory work without scientific
or practical use is incomplete (Corley and Gioia, 2011, PDF pp. 4–8, 11, 15–17).

Ask:

- What can researchers investigate or interpret better?
- What can a named actor decide or anticipate better?
- Is practical relevance intrinsic to the question or appended after the result?

### Test generativity

A broadly interesting project should often open credible further questions,
provide infrastructure or a capability others can use, attract another community,
or reorganize a research agenda. Generativity is forward-looking and differs from
realized impact (Howard-Grenville, Vasudeva, and Yiu, 2022, PDF pp. 5–7).

Ask:

- What serious follow-on questions arise without assuming the preferred result?
- Can other researchers build on the contribution?
- Does the project end in a one-off fact, or begin a line of inquiry?

Implication density is a useful early screen: ask what else follows if the claim
is right and whether the idea leads to a family of implications rather than a
dead end (Varian, 1997, PDF p. 3).

### Require result-robust importance

Separate the importance of the question from the entertainment value of a
surprising answer. A strong case explains why a conventional, null, heterogeneous,
or contrary result would still teach something consequential. Surprise is an
optional virtue, not a project-selection objective (Tihanyi, 2020, PDF pp. 1–3).

Ask:

- What is learned under the leading plausible outcomes?
- Would an unsurprising answer still matter?
- Does a null result adjudicate a real prior, or merely leave the pitch empty?

Precise competing hypotheses can distinguish genuine learning from a design in
which either sign is declared a success after the fact (Edmans, 2025, PDF
pp. 7–8). Do not audit the hypotheses' methodological correctness under this
skill.

## Criterion Two: A Sufficient Leap

A sufficient leap is a meaningful change in knowledge or use relative to the
closest work. It is not chronological firstness, an empty matrix cell, or an
unfamiliar setting by itself.

### Anchor the comparison in the closest work

Require the two or three closest papers or intellectual predecessors when
available. Compare the project with them directly rather than with a diffuse
literature (Cochrane, 2005, PDF pp. 2–3).

Ask:

- Which comparator could most plausibly make a referee say “we already know
  this”?
- What does that paper establish?
- What exact claim, mechanism, object, or capability changes here?

If no adequate closest-work evidence is available after applying the controlled
search rules, evaluate only the precision of the claimed delta. State exactly
what factual novelty remains unverified.

### Specify the knowledge change

Literal firstness is weak when an informed reader could predict the result from
known relationships. Conversely, stronger data or a sharper test can matter when
it resolves material uncertainty around an existing claim (Edmans, 2025, PDF
p. 3).

Complete this sentence:

> Before this project, the closest evidence supports ___; after this project, an
> informed reader could instead conclude or do ___ because ___.

The blank must contain a substantive update, not “this setting has not been
studied.”

### Identify the challenged premise or reorganized map

A leap often exposes a consequential assumption, shows why it is inadequate, and
constructs a defensible alternative that makes new phenomena or processes
researchable. Merely filling an omission inside the inherited frame is normally
cumulative rather than transformative (Alvesson and Sandberg, 2011, PDF pp. 1,
3–6, 10–13).

Keep the challenge bounded. Denying everything an audience believes can be
unintelligible; accepting everything yields ordinary puzzle solving. Preserve
enough continuity for the departure to be assessed (Davis, 1971, PDF p. 19;
Sandberg and Alvesson, 2011, PDF pp. 9–11, 16–18).

Ask:

- Which assumption does the closest work rely on?
- Does the project change an explanatory input into an outcome, reveal a hidden
  boundary, or reorganize the causal or conceptual map?
- Is the alternative genuinely developed, or imported ready-made from another
  tradition?

### Name the contribution architecture

Distinguish among:

- a new question or phenomenon;
- a new fact or result;
- a theory, mechanism, or conceptual reorganization;
- a measure, dataset, or institutional construction;
- a method or reusable capability;
- synthesis, integration, adjudication, or a boundary condition.

Generic novelty is too coarse. Different novelty types change downstream
knowledge in different ways, and an unexpected result alone does not establish
disruption (Leahey, Lee, and Funk, 2023, PDF pp. 2–9, 17–24).

Do not assume disruption is always superior. Consolidation, synthesis,
adjudication, and precise confirmation can be valuable contributions when that is
their true purpose (Leahey, Lee, and Funk, 2023, PDF pp. 3–5, 17–23).

### Join originality to utility

Ask both whether the project changes perception and whether that change improves
research or consequential understanding. Incremental work can be useful; a
revelatory move can be useless. A stronger leap combines originality with
scientific or practical utility (Corley and Gioia, 2011, PDF pp. 4–8).

Ask:

- What becomes newly explainable, testable, measurable, predictable, or usable?
- Does the project start or redirect a conversation rather than merely extend it?
- Why is the delta worth the audience's attention?

### Trace downstream use

A contribution has greater reach when later users can treat it as a new point of
departure. Portable methods and capabilities can redirect research because they
travel across questions and contexts, but the label “new method” is not enough;
the use pathway matters (Leahey, Lee, and Funk, 2023, PDF pp. 8–9, 17–23).

Ask:

- Who can use the contribution, and for what?
- Must every user reconstruct the same foundations, or does the project create a
  reusable capability?
- If the work is integrative, what previously separate evidence can now be
  interpreted together?

### Test the leap across plausible results

A claimed leap is fragile when it exists only under one preferred sign. Ask what
each plausible outcome changes relative to the closest work. A null can be
informative when it rejects a live mechanism or bounds an important effect; it is
not informative merely because null results are uncommon.

Use:

- preferred result → exact update;
- conventional result → exact update;
- null result → exact update;
- heterogeneous result → exact update.

If only the preferred result supports the contribution, state that the leap is
result-dependent.

### Reject vacant-cell logic

An unstudied relation may be unstudied because it is unimportant. A new country,
industry, crisis, outcome, period, or subsample does not create a contribution
unless it changes a live prior or reveals a theoretically useful contrast
(Edmans, 2025, PDF pp. 3, 5).

Novelty can also be high-risk and slow to receive recognition. Combinatorial
novelty is associated with both high and low citation tails among published work,
and its main audience may sit outside the home field; this is a reason for
calibrated uncertainty, not a reason to equate novelty with value (Wang,
Veugelers, and Stephan, 2017, PDF pp. 1–11).

Published innovative strategies are rare, higher-variance, and selected survivors;
mixed novelty embedded in established structure can outperform pure novelty.
These retrospective associations do not identify ex ante success probabilities
(Foster, Rzhetsky, and Evans, 2015, PDF pp. 13–25).

## Reconstruct the Epistemic Anatomy

Run this pass after extracting the draft and admissible closest work and before
judging either criterion. Its purpose is to connect predictions, priors, results,
knowledge changes, and stakes. Use it to delimit the paper's claim, not to audit
identification or methodological correctness.

### Trace hypothesis provenance

For each load-bearing prediction, reconstruct:

1. the minimum premises required for the prediction;
2. where each premise comes from in the candidate's stated theory, admissible
   comparator evidence, or added assumptions;
3. whether the prediction is:
   - **deductive within the candidate argument:** it follows from stated premises;
   - **an imported empirical prior:** admissible closest work supports it;
   - **abductive or ex-post:** it is offered to explain a result but was not
     previously implied by the candidate's stated premises; or
   - **unknown:** the documents do not establish its provenance; and
4. whether other passages or reported results imply a conflicting prediction.

Do not infer chronology, preregistration, or author intent. If the supplied
record does not establish that a prediction was ex ante, call its provenance
unknown rather than accusing the authors of post-hoc reasoning. Precise competing
predictions make clear what different outcomes teach; a design in which every
sign is rationalized afterward does not (Edmans, 2025, PDF pp. 7–8). A claimed
assumption challenge must identify both the inherited premise and the developed
alternative rather than importing an explanation after the fact (Alvesson and
Sandberg, 2011, PDF pp. 3–6, 10–13).

Ask:

- What minimally must be true for this prediction to follow?
- Would the stated premises have predicted the sign, timing, allocation,
  intensive or extensive margin, and heterogeneity before the result was known?
- Does the draft predict mutually incompatible responses in different sections?

### Separate the relevant counterfactuals

Distinguish three objects:

- **Treatment counterfactual:** the focal treatment or exposure versus its
  absence.
- **Mechanism or design counterfactual:** the focal treatment versus an
  economically relevant alternative that separates the proposed distinctive
  feature from the broader treatment bundle.
- **Intellectual counterfactual:** what an informed reader would believe without
  the project.

Use the first two only to bound the contribution's wording. Do not assess whether
the empirical design validly identifies them. If the supplied evidence compares
treatment with no treatment but not with an equivalent alternative, credit the
treatment-bundle result but do not credit a claim that one design feature is
uniquely responsible. The intellectual counterfactual supplies the before-state
for a genuine belief update (Davis, 1971, PDF pp. 1, 3–5; Edmans, 2025, PDF
pp. 3–5).

### Map the prior across dimensions

Do not reduce the informed prior to the expected sign of one coefficient. When
the admissible candidate and comparator evidence permits, reconstruct separate
priors for:

- sign and economically relevant magnitude;
- timing and persistence;
- allocation, substitution, or crowd-out;
- intensive and extensive margins;
- heterogeneity;
- mechanism; and
- risk, harm, welfare, or policy consequences.

For each load-bearing question, map:

| Question | Informed prior from admissible comparator evidence | Candidate result | Exact knowledge update |
|---|---|---|---|

An expected sign can coexist with material uncertainty about margins, timing, or
mechanism. The contribution may therefore reside in a secondary result rather
than the headline coefficient. Conversely, a surprising sign is not a useful
update unless it changes a consequential belief or use (Tihanyi, 2020, PDF
pp. 1–3; Edmans, 2025, PDF pp. 3–5).

### Identify the contribution-bearing result

Distinguish:

- the headline result;
- the most surprising result;
- the result that carries the claimed knowledge change; and
- the result that carries the scientific, economic, or practical stakes.

These need not be the same. Ask which finding survives an informed reader's
“of course” objection. Then state why that finding, rather than mere firstness or
the headline sign, creates a new interpretation, boundary, adjudication, or
capability (Edmans, 2025, PDF p. 3; Leahey, Lee, and Funk, 2023, PDF pp. 2–9,
17–24).

### Assign comparator roles when no single twin exists

If one closest paper clearly dominates, compare against it directly. If the
candidate sits between literatures and no single paper is a plausible twin, use
only admissible candidate-specific comparator evidence to identify a small set
with distinct roles:

- **treatment or product sibling:** the same input with another outcome;
- **conceptual predecessor:** the same mechanism or economic logic; and
- **outcome or method mirror:** the same outcome or design, possibly with the
  causal arrow reversed.

State the delta against each comparator. A triangular comparison can locate a
contribution, but combining literatures or filling their intersection is not by
itself a leap (Cochrane, 2005, PDF pp. 2–3; Edmans, 2025, PDF pp. 3, 5).

### Trace the claim ladder

Separate the candidate's:

> measured outcome → behaviour → risk or harm → welfare → policy implication

Mark every transition as established, claimed, inferred, or unknown from the
supplied record. Do not let a consequential topic silently convert an observed
outcome into realized harm, net welfare, or an optimal policy. Scientific and
practical utility require a defensible use pathway, while claims about net value
must address relevant benefits and costs (Tihanyi, 2020, PDF pp. 1–3; Corley and
Gioia, 2011, PDF pp. 4–8, 11, 15–17; Edmans, 2025, PDF p. 15).

## Interaction Between the Criteria

Explain the interaction in prose:

- When the stakes are broad but the delta is small, the project may document an
  important setting without changing what is known.
- When the delta is large but the stakes are narrow, the project may be
  intellectually original without a compelling reason for a broad audience to
  care.
- When both cases rely on the same mechanism, show how the knowledge change
  affects a consequential audience.
- When they rely on different mechanisms, state the tension rather than forcing
  a unified story.

Prepared problem choice adds a practical distinction: keep consequential
questions without a plausible attack on a monitored horizon, and commit when an
attack becomes visible (Kaiser/Hamming, 2010, PDF pp. 6–9). Feasibility itself is
outside this skill, but the distinction prevents admiration for a question from
being mistaken for a completed contribution case.

Question choice is also a portfolio decision. Compare candidate ideas on expected
knowledge gain and researcher fit rather than committing to the first available
project, while recognizing that this does not establish novelty or importance
(Alon, 2009, PDF pp. 1–3). Unusual questions can impose extra legitimation and
career costs, so distinguish a weak scholarly case from a strong case with
institutional risk (Niederle, 2011, PDF pp. 1–4).

## False Proxies to Challenge

| Proxy | Why it fails | Required replacement |
|---|---|---|
| A large, urgent, or fashionable topic | The focal relation may still be second-order | Name the specific affected outcome, audience, and decision (Tihanyi, 2020, PDF pp. 1–3; Edmans, 2025, PDF p. 15) |
| A surprising sign or counterintuitive coefficient | Surprise can be trivial and result-dependent | State the prior, consequence, and learning under other plausible results (Tihanyi, 2020, PDF pp. 1–3; Leahey, Lee, and Funk, 2023, PDF pp. 6, 16–23) |
| A new dataset, country, period, platform, or event | A setting is not a knowledge change | Explain the theoretically useful contrast or uncertainty resolved (Edmans, 2025, PDF pp. 3, 6–8) |
| Technical difficulty or sophistication | Effort and complexity do not establish contribution | State the insight or capability created; use the simplest adequate expression (Cochrane, 2005, PDF pp. 2–6) |
| An empty literature cell | The cell may be empty because nobody should care | Show the informed prior and first-order consequence (Edmans, 2025, PDF p. 5) |
| Novelty by itself | Originality can lack utility; novelty has variable downstream effects | Join originality to scientific or practical use (Corley and Gioia, 2011, PDF pp. 4–8; Wang, Veugelers, and Stephan, 2017, PDF pp. 1–11) |
| A broad or prestigious target outlet | Outlet ambition does not make the question broad | Identify the natural audience and why it would read the abstract (Edmans, 2025, PDF pp. 5–6) |
| Many possible follow-up analyses | Volume is not generativity | Name distinct questions or capabilities others can pursue (Howard-Grenville, Vasudeva, and Yiu, 2022, PDF pp. 5–7) |

## Corpus-to-Diagnostic Map

Use the full corpus as a set of complementary lenses:

- Alon (2009): knowledge gain, researcher fit, and delayed commitment
  (PDF pp. 1–3).
- Alvesson and Sandberg (2011): consequential assumption challenge and a
  non-stock alternative (PDF pp. 1, 3–6, 10–13, 20–21).
- Alvesson and Sandberg (2020): question and literature domain as revisable
  outputs of deep reading (PDF pp. 2–13).
- Bartunek, Rynes, and Ireland (2006): importance and credibility precede
  audience-relative interestingness (PDF pp. 2–6).
- Cochrane (2005): compress the contribution and compare it with the closest
  papers (PDF pp. 2–3).
- Colquitt and George (2011): significance, conversation change, curiosity,
  scope, and actionability (PDF pp. 1–3).
- Corley and Gioia (2011): originality joined to scientific and practical
  utility (PDF pp. 4–8, 11–17).
- Davis (1971): explicit audience prior and bounded reversal (PDF pp. 1–5,
  19–26).
- Edmans (2025): belief update, first-order importance, audience reach, and
  vacant-cell rejection (PDF pp. 2–8, 15, 21–22).
- Foster, Rzhetsky, and Evans (2015): forms of novelty, survivor selection, and
  risk/reward (PDF pp. 13–25).
- Howard-Grenville, Vasudeva, and Yiu (2022): importance, interestingness,
  generativity, and realized impact are distinct (PDF pp. 1–7).
- Kaiser/Hamming (2010): consequential problems, attackability, and cumulative
  reframing (PDF pp. 6–9).
- Leahey, Lee, and Funk (2023): contribution architecture and the distinction
  between consolidation and disruption (PDF pp. 2–9, 17–24).
- Niederle (2011): breadth, external intelligibility, agenda formation, and the
  costs of unusual topics (PDF pp. 1–4).
- Sandberg and Alvesson (2011): gap-spotting versus bounded assumption challenge
  (PDF pp. 9–11, 16–18).
- Tihanyi (2020): consequential problems before surprise (PDF pp. 1–3).
- Varian (1997): cheap prototyping, implication density, and mechanism clarity
  (PDF pp. 2–9).
- Wang, Veugelers, and Stephan (2017): novelty as a high-variance,
  delayed-recognition signal rather than a guarantee (PDF pp. 1–11).
