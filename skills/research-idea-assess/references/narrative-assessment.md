## Governing Form

Return a reasoned narrative after the user has confirmed or corrected the
strongest defensible formulation. Do not use numerical scores, traffic lights,
rankings, weighted rubrics, or fixed verdict categories.

Make the assessment decisive where the evidence permits and explicit where it
does not. Distinguish:

- what the project materials establish;
- what the author claims;
- what the assessment infers;
- what supplied or searched comparator evidence establishes; and
- what remains unverified.

## Required Structure

Use the following headings in this order.

### Bottom line

State the overall judgment in a short paragraph. Describe the relation between
the two criteria rather than assigning a label.

Identify the contribution-bearing result when it differs from the headline
result. If a load-bearing explanation is deductive, imported from prior evidence,
abductive/ex-post, or of unknown provenance, state that distinction when it
changes the judgment.

Useful forms include:

- “As currently framed, the project makes a clearer case for broad interest than
  for a sufficient leap because …”
- “The claimed leap is intelligible, but the case for broad interest depends on
  …”
- “The same mechanism supports both breadth and contribution: …”
- “The project may be worth pursuing for a narrower audience, but the present
  general-interest claim rests on …”

Avoid “strong,” “weak,” “high,” or “low” as standalone categories. Explain the
substance.

### Strongest version of the question and contribution

Restate the corrected formulation. Include:

- the exact question;
- the intended audience;
- the consequential stake;
- the informed prior and intellectual counterfactual;
- the treatment and any distinct mechanism/design counterfactual relevant to the
  contribution's wording;
- the closest-work baseline; and
- the contribution-bearing result and exact knowledge, interpretation, or
  capability change.

Do not improve the claim beyond what the user confirmed.

### The case for and against broad interest

Present the strongest supporting case and the strongest countercase. Address the
dimensions that matter for this project:

- affected audiences;
- informed priors across the relevant sign, timing, allocation, margin,
  heterogeneity, mechanism, and welfare dimensions;
- scientific or economic stakes;
- scope beyond the immediate setting;
- scientific or practical utility;
- generativity; and
- whether conventional or null results would still matter.

Where the stakes depend on a chain from measured outcome to behaviour, harm,
welfare, or policy, distinguish which transitions the supplied evidence
establishes and which remain claimed or inferred.

Do not force every dimension into equal space. Focus on the load-bearing
premises.

### The case for and against a sufficient leap

Present the strongest supporting case and countercase. Address:

- the admissible closest work and distinct comparator roles when no single paper
  is a twin;
- the contribution-bearing result, the exact knowledge change, and why it
  survives the informed reader's “of course” objection;
- the provenance and minimum premises of any load-bearing hypothesis;
- the treatment, mechanism/design, and intellectual counterfactuals;
- any challenged assumption or reorganized map;
- contribution type;
- originality joined to utility;
- downstream use;
- result dependence; and
- whether the claim is more than a vacant literature cell.

When no adequate comparator evidence is available, say:

> The contribution is assessable as an articulated claim, but factual novelty
> remains unverified for [exact claim or scope] because [specific missing
> comparator coverage].

Fill both brackets from the evidence boundary. For a source-bounded assessment
with no closest papers, the cause is that no closest-work evidence was supplied.
For a failed or partial search, name the missing field, source type, layer, date
range, validation failure, or unresolved item. Do not soften or omit the sentence
merely because the pitch sounds plausible.

### How the two criteria interact

Explain whether the same feature supports both criteria, whether one criterion
limits the other, or whether the project contains a productive tension.
State explicitly when the result that carries the knowledge change differs from
the result or claim that carries the stakes.

Examples:

- an important institutional problem with only a setting extension;
- a reusable method with broad scientific utility but a narrow motivating
  application;
- a surprising result with little result-robust learning;
- an incremental estimate that materially resolves uncertainty in a first-order
  debate; or
- an assumption challenge whose alternative opens a consequential research
  programme.

Do not average the criteria into one verdict.

### Claims that remain unverified

List only material uncertainties. Typical items include:

- factual novelty relative to literature outside the admissible comparator
  coverage;
- the audience's actual prior;
- whether a hypothesis was genuinely implied before the result or is an
  abductive/ex-post interpretation;
- whether the evidence distinguishes a claimed design feature from the broader
  treatment bundle;
- the scale or breadth of the affected outcome;
- unsupported transitions from a measured outcome to behaviour, harm, welfare,
  or policy;
- whether follow-on users exist;
- whether a setting difference identifies a meaningful boundary; or
- whether the claimed contribution survives conventional or null results.

Do not include methodological correctness here. State once, if relevant:

> Identification, measurement, methods, and execution were assumed rather than
> audited, as required by this assessment's scope.

### Most informative next revision or evidence request

Give one action with the highest expected value for changing the assessment.
Make it concrete.

Good examples:

- rewrite the contribution as a before/after knowledge claim against one named
  closest paper;
- rewrite the conceptual framework around explicit competing priors for sign,
  timing, allocation, margins, and mechanism;
- either derive a headline interpretation from stated premises or label it as an
  abductive interpretation of the result;
- narrow a unique-design claim to the treatment bundle actually compared;
- identify the contribution-bearing result that survives the closest
  comparator's “of course” objection;
- supply and annotate the two closest papers;
- show which first-order decision changes under a conventional result;
- replace a country novelty claim with a theoretically useful boundary claim;
- map the follow-on questions that do not depend on the headline sign; or
- identify the audience prior using evidence already in the supplied materials.

Avoid generic lists such as “strengthen the motivation, literature review, and
contribution.”

### Source basis

Separate sources into two groups:

1. **Framework basis:** cite the load-bearing framework claims by author-year and
   physical PDF page.
2. **Candidate basis:** cite the user's idea and draft.
3. **Comparator basis:** cite supplied closest papers and any searched
   comparators separately. If `search-literature` ran or a prior map was reused,
   report the search date, fields, layers, coverage limits, and paths to
   `literature-search-map.md` and `literature-search-log.md`. Cite the log only
   for relevant validation or reference-verification evidence, not as a raw
   search transcript.

End with:

> This is an expert-derived diagnostic, not a validated predictor of publication
> or impact. Candidate-specific comparators came only from [supplied closest
> work / the auditable search-literature map / both / none under the stated
> source boundary]; no ad hoc outside literature was introduced.

Adapt the bracket to the facts. For a broad-interest-only assessment, state that
sufficient leap was not assessed and no comparator search was needed.

## Writing Rules

- Lead with the assessment, not a recap of the dialogue.
- Prefer connected prose to a checklist.
- Quote sparingly and only when the exact wording is analytically important.
- Preserve disagreement and uncertainty.
- Do not praise ambition as evidence.
- Do not infer factual novelty from absence in the user's description.
- Do not call a prediction ex ante, deductive, or preregistered unless the
  supplied record establishes that status.
- Do not equate a measured outcome with behaviour, harm, welfare, or a policy
  conclusion without tracing the supplied support for each transition.
- Do not credit a unique mechanism or design feature when the supplied
  counterfactual compares only the broader treatment bundle with no treatment.
- Do not smuggle methodological criticism into contribution language.
- Do not imply that an external search occurred unless an adequate
  `search-literature` map and log exist.
- Do not convert the framework into publication advice unless the requested
  audience or outlet makes fit part of the broad-interest case.
- Keep the answer proportionate to the materials: concise for an idea, fuller for
  a developed draft and adequate supplied or searched comparators.

## Source Attribution Examples

Framework:

> A new country or period does not itself establish a knowledge change; the
> project must alter an informed prior or reveal a useful boundary (Edmans, 2025,
> PDF p. 3).

Candidate:

> The draft claims that the reform changes household refinancing incentives
> (`proposal.pdf`, p. 4).

Closest work:

> The supplied comparator estimates the average uptake effect but does not model
> the proposed liquidity mechanism (Author, year, supplied PDF pp. 12–14).

Searched comparator:

> The closest mapped precedent estimates the same treatment-outcome relation but
> not the candidate's allocation mechanism (`literature-search-map.md`, “Closest
> Published Precedents”, verified DOI).

Inference:

> On the supplied record, the setting is best interpreted as a boundary test
> rather than as a standalone source of novelty (assessment inference).
