# Research Idea — Frame

## Objective

Move from a narrow topic, association, result, or setting to a consequential problem that the available evidence can illuminate. Recover the economic uncertainty beneath the application instead of replacing specific nouns with broader nouns.

Treat a larger frame as substantive when it changes or sharpens at least one of:

- the knowledge claim or prior being revised;
- the relevance warrant connecting the setting to the problem;
- the mechanism or economic interpretation;
- the estimand, construct, or evidence burden;
- the scope of the claim; or
- the order in which the paper develops its argument.

A broader label that changes none of these is cosmetic. A new frame will often require new tests, but an unchanged estimate can sometimes bear on a larger problem through a new, defensible warrant.

## Keep The Roles Distinct

Use four core roles:

- **X — Problem or puzzle:** an unresolved condition and the more consequential understanding or decision that remains blocked because of it.
- **Y — Research question:** the specific, answerable question through which the paper reduces the uncertainty in X.
- **Z — Importance:** why resolving that blockage changes an informed reader’s belief, explanation, prediction, allocation, or decision.
- **S — Setting:** the empirical or institutional context that gives unusual leverage on Y.

Use this relationship:

> Because ___ remains unresolved, we cannot yet understand or judge ___: this is **X**. We study X by asking **Y**. Answering Y matters because **Z**. We use **S** because it relaxes ___ and provides the following diagnostic contrast: ___.

Do not relabel X as the “research program” or Y as the “paper-level question.” Those phrases describe scale, not function. Keep X as the whole consequential problem and use Y as the tractable slice. State how Y informs X and what remains unresolved.

Keep the result separate. If known, state **A — Answer or knowledge change** only after X, Y, Z, and S are clear.

## Use Two Passes

### Discover Bottom-Up

Work from:

> finding or setting → distinctive feature → economic function or interpretation → affected boundary or incidence → blocked understanding → problem X

Post-result framing is legitimate recursive inquiry. Record that provenance and use the resulting frame to organise interpretation and motivate fresh tests; do not retroactively present exploratory patterns as confirmatory hypotheses.

### Explain Top-Down

Write or outline:

> target prior → unresolved condition and consequence → X → Y → relevance warrant → diagnostic value of S → A and contribution

Lead with the destination rather than the chronology of discovery.

## Workflow

### 1. Reconstruct The Starting Point And Binding Constraint

Extract:

- the relationship, treatment, institutional feature, outcome, and population;
- the estimand and result, if known;
- the inherited framing;
- the chronology of question, analysis, and interpretation; and
- what is genuinely puzzling rather than merely new.

Diagnose whether the main weakness is:

- **contribution or framing:** no consequential prior or blocked understanding;
- **execution:** the evidence cannot answer the proposed question; or
- **exposition:** the contribution exists but readers cannot recover it.

Do not promise that broader wording can repair an execution failure. Read supplied material before asking questions. Ask at most one question at a time, only when it would materially change X, Y, or Z.

### 2. Specify The Reader, Prior, And Problem Consequence

Complete:

```text
Target informed reader:
Relevant prior or accepted account:
Unresolved condition:
Blocked understanding or decision (Q2):
What an answer could change:
Material magnitude, if relevant:
```

Define X from the unresolved condition and blocked consequence together. Do not shrink X to match the available outcome.

Distinguish:

- **conceptual significance:** answering Y helps resolve a more important understanding;
- **practical significance:** a named actor could make a different decision after learning A.

For a practical Z, state who decides what differently. Treat policy relevance without a plausible knowledge-to-action link as a bounded implication, not the source of importance.

### 3. Locate The Reference Class, Tension, And Functions

Place the focal object in a familiar economic class such as consumer credit, ownership, insurance, payment systems, labour contracts, disclosure, platforms, or markets.

Identify a live tension, for example:

- access versus protection;
- efficiency versus redistribution;
- value creation versus stakeholder extraction;
- contract performance versus household welfare;
- individual optimisation versus coordination; or
- competition versus exploitation.

When the setting changes a familiar institutional bundle, compare only relevant dimensions:

| Dimension | Reference form | Removed, weakened, relocated, or intensified |

| Price or application |  |  |
| Information or disclosure |  |  |
| Screening or eligibility |  |  |
| Contract or account structure |  |  |
| Monitoring or reporting |  |  |
| Ownership or incentives |  |  |

For each material difference, ask what function it might perform, whose objective it serves, and whether success should be judged at the transaction, contract, firm, consumer, household, market, or system level.

Use **functional inversion**: reconsider an apparent inefficiency as a possible safeguard, or an apparent protection as a possible barrier.

### 4. Identify The Knowledge Bottleneck And Framing Warrant

State why existing theory or evidence cannot settle Y. Candidate bottlenecks include:

- missing outcomes or counterparties;
- aggregation or an inappropriate unit of analysis;
- selection or lack of a credible counterfactual;
- poor construct measurement;
- bundled institutional changes; or
- inability to observe the relevant boundary.

Then identify which feature of S relaxes that specific constraint. If S relaxes none, it is merely a setting.

Write the framing warrant:

> Evidence from S informs X because, when **[general structural condition]**, **[the observed contrast]** is diagnostic of **[the broader mechanism, interpretation, or consequence]**.

Test whether:

1. S is genuinely an instance of the general condition;
2. A and the broader conclusion are instances of the warranted relation;
3. a competing warrant gives the same evidence a different meaning; and
4. the warrant states its scope.

Audit the full relevance chain:

> S and evidence → A → Y → X → Z

Require a defensible inference at every arrow.

### 5. Test Rival Interpretations, Answer Space, And Provenance

State the strongest credible alternative, null, or objection. Add more only when genuinely live; do not manufacture symmetric theories.

Test whether the question matters across its answer space:

| Possible answer | What belief, explanation, or decision changes? | Worth knowing? |
|---|---|---|
| Expected |  |  |
| Null |  |  |
| Opposite |  |  |

Reject result-dependent importance that exists only because the realised coefficient was surprising.

Record:

```text
Ex ante prediction, if any:
Result-generated interpretation, if any:
Fresh or holdout evidence required:
Preferred interpretation and decisive reason:
```

After considering alternatives, state the interpretation best supported by the evidence. Do not use calibration as an excuse to leave the paper’s posterior implicit.

### 6. Build The X–Y–Z–S Map

Complete:

```text
Problem or puzzle X:
Research question Y:
Importance Z:
Setting S:
Knowledge bottleneck:
Framing warrant:
Diagnostic contrast:
Answer or knowledge change A, if known:
Epistemic provenance:
Unresolved remainder:
```

Require:

- X to contain the unresolved condition and blocked consequence;
- Y to be a tractable question that genuinely informs X;
- Z to identify a consequential prior or decision that could change;
- S to relax the stated bottleneck;
- the warrant to explain why evidence from S travels to X; and
- the proposed evidence to distinguish at least one live interpretation.

Remove the setting’s proper nouns and current policy event. If X then ceases to be economically meaningful, the frame is probably setting-led.

### 7. Build A Mechanism Or Interpretation Bridge

Use a causal mechanism bridge only when the paper makes an explanatory claim:

> feature or shock → actor objective or constraint → choice margin → conduct → outcome → incidence

Specify the actor, objective, choice margin, heterogeneity, transmission, observable footprints, and alternatives.

For descriptive or correlational contributions, do not invent a causal channel. Instead map:

> measured fact → economic construct it captures → rival meanings → downstream implication

Distinguish:

1. evidence against alternative A;
2. evidence consistent with preferred B; and
3. direct positive evidence for B.

Reject “not A, therefore B.” Require positive mechanism evidence only when claiming B. Do not demand elaborate channel tests when distinguishing channels would not change the knowledge claim or decision.

### 8. Audit Construct, Estimand, And Incidence

Identify exactly what moves and for whom:

| Question | Required answer |
|---|---|
| Construct | concept named in Y |
| Measure | outcome actually observed and why it captures the construct |
| Unit and object | transaction, firm, household; level, share, frequency, duration, or total |
| Margin and horizon | extensive, intensive, composition, timing, persistence; short or long run |
| Accounting | creation, destruction, displacement, transfer, or redistribution |
| Counterpart | who supplies the gain or bears the loss |
| Distribution | which groups experience different effects |
| Unmeasured component | part of Y the evidence does not capture |
| Net question | benefits and costs required to assess value, protection, affordability, or welfare |

An observation becomes evidence for a framing claim only when the measure captures the named construct and the result discriminates among relevant interpretations.

Do not infer an aggregate change from a share change, consumer harm from firm capture or expenditure, or affordability from repayment. Evidence on one side of a trade-off cannot answer a net-value question.

### 9. Write The Evidence Contract, Scope, And Claim Ceiling

For each important claim, complete:

| Claim | Empirical implication | Existing evidence | Missing evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
|  |  |  |  | established / supported / speculative |

Ask what would falsify or materially weaken the frame. Treat it as substantive when it changes the knowledge claim, relevance warrant, evidence burden, interpretation, scope, or exposition. Otherwise label it cosmetic.

Define:

- **essential features:** conditions required for the warrant or mechanism;
- **incidental features:** facts peculiar to S;
- **portable class:** settings sharing the essential structure.

Then separate what the estimate establishes, the interpretation it supports, mechanisms it does not isolate, causal claims requiring more identification, welfare claims requiring more outcomes, compositional effects from aggregate changes, and generalisations resting on untested scope conditions.

### 10. Design The Exposition

Recommend:

1. establish the target prior and consequential phenomenon;
2. state the unresolved condition and blocked consequence;
3. introduce X, Y, and Z;
4. give the relevance warrant and bottleneck;
5. explain why S is diagnostic;
6. present A, its interpretation, and the evidence contract;
7. state scope and the claim ceiling.

Order literatures by role:

1. **problem-defining or home literature;**
2. **mechanism or interpretation bridge;**
3. **setting and measurement literature.**

Audit propagation through the title, abstract, introduction, literature order, framework, empirical tests, and conclusion. A frame discovered after the results may legitimately reorganise the paper and motivate new analysis, but it cannot retroactively convert exploratory evidence into confirmation.

## Default Output

Lead with the destination. By default return:

1. **Core frame:** a short plain-language account plus X–Y–Z–S–A.
2. **Why it is informative:** target prior, blocked consequence, knowledge bottleneck, and framing warrant.
3. **Empirical contract:** construct and estimand alignment, mechanism or interpretation, missing evidence, scope, and claim ceiling.
4. **Cognitive route:** include when the user asks how the frame was discovered or wants to learn the tool.

Expand the answer-space test, evidence table, or exposition blueprint only when useful. If several genuinely different problems fit, present no more than three and identify the decision separating them.

## Guardrails

- Reject category ascent that merely substitutes “unintended consequences,” “innovation,” “ownership,” or another capacious label.
- Do not call X a research program or shrink it to the easiest measurable outcome.
- Do not treat scale, novelty, topicality, regulation, an empty literature cell, or unusual data as Z.
- Do not call a setting diagnostic unless it relaxes a named knowledge bottleneck and passes a relevance warrant.
- Do not present a result-generated interpretation as an ex ante hypothesis.
- Do not force a causal mechanism onto an important descriptive fact.
- Do not equate data with evidence, a proxy with its construct, or one side of a trade-off with net value.
- Do not infer the function of one feature when the setting changes a bundle.
- Do not equate evidence against one mechanism with evidence for another.
- Do not infer aggregate creation from reallocation or harm from behaviour, expenditure, repayment, or firm capture alone.
- Do not call researcher co-observation consumer salience.
- Do not generalise through an industry list without a shared warrant and scope conditions.
- Do not invoke, hand off to, or depend on another skill.
- Do not run a novelty or literature search as part of framing. If a broader
  request separately includes discovery, keep its evidence and claims distinct.
- Use assessment and writing skills for broad-interest evaluation and polished prose; keep this skill focused on framing logic.
